About This Portfolio

Background and purpose

My background

I am currently studying Business Information Technology at Haaga-Helia University of Applied Sciences in Finland. My previous experience includes sales, marketing, and operations management. Because of this background, I am especially interested in how digital services can improve everyday experiences, solve practical problems, and create value through better user experience.

Purpose of this portfolio

This portfolio documents my learning journey throughout the course. It brings together reflections on self-introduction, CSS basics, web portfolio development, UX principles, digital designer work, complexity in design, mapping methods, journey mapping, UI principles, and final self and peer reflection.

Learning Journey

Course learning and development

Start

Starting point and technical foundations

Self-introduction, CSS basics, and building a web portfolio

Learning goals and personal direction

At the beginning of the course, I identified my background in business, operations, and communication as strengths, but I also recognized that I needed deeper knowledge of UX methods, digital service design processes, and practical prototyping. I was especially interested in learning how digital services are designed from a user-centered perspective and how design decisions can be justified through research rather than assumptions.

My goal was not only to understand digital service design theoretically, but also to apply it in practice through project work. I wanted to become more confident in using design methods such as journey mapping, sketching, prototyping, and usability thinking.

CSS and web portfolio foundations

The CSS tutorial helped me understand how visual structure is created on the web through layout, spacing, typography, colors, and hierarchy. Even though CSS may seem technical at first, I realized that it directly supports user experience because it affects readability, consistency, and clarity.

Building a team web portfolio also highlighted the importance of structure, coherence, and communication in digital presentation. A portfolio is not only a collection of content, but also a user experience in itself. It should help the viewer navigate, understand priorities, and connect content into a meaningful whole.

UX

UX principles and the role of design

Jakob Nielsen, digital designer work, and Don Norman’s view on complexity

Jakob Nielsen and usability principles

Jakob Nielsen’s usability principles made a strong impression on me because they showed that good UX is based on clear logic, not only visual taste. Principles such as visibility of system status, consistency, error prevention, and user control provide a practical framework for evaluating whether a design truly supports the user.

In my Student Well-being App project, these principles influenced the way I thought about navigation, visual hierarchy, and clarity of interaction. I learned that an interface may look clean, but if the user does not understand what is happening or what to do next, the design is still weak.

Digital designer work in practice

The video about working as a digital designer helped me understand that digital design is not isolated creative work. It is strongly connected with teamwork, iteration, communication, and business context. Designers collaborate with developers, product owners, and other stakeholders, which means they must explain design decisions clearly and adapt to real constraints.

This reflection resonated with my own background because I value both practical problem-solving and communication. It also helped me see that my future path could connect business understanding with UX thinking.

Living with complexity

Don Norman’s lecture helped me think more critically about complexity in digital systems. One of the most important takeaways for me was that complexity cannot always be removed. Instead, design should help people understand and manage it.

This idea was highly relevant to our well-being app project. We could have added many features, but doing so would likely have made the experience more overwhelming. Instead, we focused on a smaller set of useful interactions. This taught me that good design is often about prioritization and reduction with purpose, not simplification for its own sake.

Process

Design process and service thinking

Mapping, sketching, decision-making, journey mapping, and UI design principles

Mapping, sketching, and deciding

The Map, Sketch, and Decide phases gave a clear structure to early design work. Mapping helped identify the overall problem space and user journey. Sketching encouraged exploration and creativity without committing too early. Deciding helped narrow down ideas and focus on a concept that best matched user needs.

I found this process especially valuable because it prevented random decision-making. Instead of jumping directly into screens, we first explored the logic of the experience. This changed how I think about design work: good design begins with understanding, not decoration.

Journey mapping

Journey mapping helped me understand that user experience happens over time through multiple steps, touchpoints, and emotions. In our project, journey mapping helped visualize how a student might open the app, record their mood, receive support, and review weekly progress.

This method was useful because it revealed not only what users do, but also what they may feel or struggle with during the process. It showed me that design should support the entire flow of use, not only individual screens.

UI design principles

This part of the course helped me define my own checklist for evaluating interface quality. The most important criteria I selected were: consistency, clear hierarchy, readable typography, intuitive navigation, accessible contrast, and emotional appropriateness.

I chose these principles because they directly affect usability and trust. In the Student Well-being App project, these ideas shaped the use of soft color palettes, rounded components, friendly visuals, and a calm interface structure. I learned that good UI is not only visually pleasing; it actively supports a more comfortable and understandable experience.

Prototype Project

Student Well-being App

A practical application of the course concepts in a real design case focused on student well-being, emotional support, and simple daily interaction.

Project overview

The Student Well-being App was designed to help students monitor daily mood, energy, sleep, and activity in a simple, supportive, and non-overwhelming way. The concept focuses on emotional accessibility and gentle habit support.

Tools used in the project included Figma for prototyping, Canva for visual storytelling, user surveys for gathering insights, and prototype-based reflection to improve interaction clarity.

Key design insight

One of the most important insights from this project was that students do not necessarily need more information; they need better support and lower friction. That realization shaped the design toward small daily actions, clear screens, positive feedback, and emotionally calm interaction.

Final Reflection

Learning outcomes, critical thinking, and assessment

Critical Reflection 1 – Simplicity requires judgment

One of the strongest lessons from this course is that simplicity should not be confused with reducing everything. In practice, oversimplification can also weaken a service if essential value is removed. Through the project, I learned that meaningful simplicity comes from prioritizing what matters most to the user. This is a more critical and disciplined view of design than simply aiming for minimal appearance.

Critical Reflection 2 – Design decisions should be justified academically

Another major learning point was the importance of evidence-based design. During the project, some ideas seemed attractive, but they were not always the clearest or most useful. This experience showed me that design decisions should be supported by user needs, design principles, and testing logic rather than by personal taste alone. This changed the way I understand professional and academic quality in UX work.

Critical Reflection 3 – Emotional design is part of usability

Before this course, I mostly saw usability as task completion and interface clarity. Now I understand that emotional tone is also part of usability. In a well-being app, users must feel supported, not judged. This insight expanded my view of UX: digital services should not only function efficiently, but also respond to human emotion, motivation, and vulnerability.

Self-assessment

My contribution to the course work included idea development, UX reflection, portfolio structuring, and connecting user needs with practical design choices. I believe my strongest improvement was in reflective thinking: I became more capable of explaining why a design choice matters and how it connects to user experience.

Compared with the beginning of the course, I now have a stronger foundation in UX concepts, digital service thinking, and design communication. I also feel more confident using UX language in a structured and critical way.

Peer and team reflection

Working in a team showed me that good digital design depends on collaboration and alignment, not only individual creativity. Different members contributed different strengths, and this reflected real project work. I learned that the quality of a design outcome depends not only on the idea itself, but also on how clearly a team shares direction, feedback, and responsibility.

Overall, this course successfully connected theory and application. That made the learning process much more meaningful for me.
